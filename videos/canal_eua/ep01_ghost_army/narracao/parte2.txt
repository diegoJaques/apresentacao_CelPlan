The Ghost Army fought with three kinds of weapons. None of them could fire a single bullet.

The first weapon was what the enemy could see.

The largest part of the unit was the Six Hundred and Third Engineer Camouflage Battalion. These were the artists. Their tools were inflatable decoys: rubber tanks, trucks, jeeps and artillery guns, each one folded into a bag and blown up with a compressor in a matter of minutes.

From the air, through a pair of binoculars, or in a reconnaissance photograph, a field full of these decoys looked exactly like an armored division getting ready for battle.

But the artists knew that an army is more than its vehicles. Real tanks leave tracks in the mud. Real soldiers leave trash, laundry, and campfire smoke. So the men of the Ghost Army made those, too. They used vehicles, and even bulldozers, to draw tank tracks across the fields. They placed decoys with care, as if a real crew had parked them in a hurry.

The second weapon was what the enemy could hear.

A separate company, the Thirty-One Thirty-Second Signal Service Company, had trained in secret in upstate New York. Working with engineers from Bell Labs, they had recorded the sounds of real American units: tanks rolling, trucks shifting gears, men building bridges, officers shouting orders.

They mixed those sounds into programs, like a radio play, and stored them on wire recorders, the most advanced recording technology of the day.

At night, they drove half-tracks close to the front, carrying enormous five-hundred-watt speakers. And then they pressed play.

On a quiet night, those sounds could carry for miles, some say as far as fifteen miles. To a German soldier listening in the dark, it sounded like an entire armored column was moving into position.

The third weapon was what the enemy could intercept.

The Signal Company Special handled the radio. Every army listens to its enemy's radio traffic. The Ghost Army used that against the Germans. Its operators sent fake messages, imitating the real units they were pretending to be.

They even learned to copy the personal rhythm of other operators… the way each man tapped out Morse code, almost like handwriting. So that a German listener, hearing a familiar "hand" on the air, would never suspect a thing.

And there was a fourth weapon, maybe the most human of all: theater.

Members of the Ghost Army sewed on the shoulder patches of other divisions. They painted fake unit markings on their trucks. Some of them went into nearby towns and sat in cafés, talking a little too loudly about where "their division" was headed next, knowing that spies might be listening.

A few officers even played the part of generals, riding through villages with the right flags on their cars.

Sight. Sound. Radio. And a bit of acting. Put together, it was the first traveling, multimedia deception unit in the history of the United States Army.
