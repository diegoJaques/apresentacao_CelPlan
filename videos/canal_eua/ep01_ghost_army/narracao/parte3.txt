In the spring of nineteen forty-four, the Ghost Army crossed the Atlantic. Some of its men landed in France in the weeks after D-Day, while the fighting in Normandy was still raging.

Their first missions were small, and they learned quickly. A decoy placed in the wrong spot could give the whole game away. A rubber tank left in the sun could slowly sag. A careless remark in a village could undo a week of work.

Every operation became a production, with a script, a set, a cast and a schedule. The men joked that they were a traveling road show. In a way, they were.

And their stages were never far from real danger. To be convincing, the Ghost Army often had to set up close to the front line, sometimes closer than the units they were imitating. They had almost no real weapons to defend themselves. Their protection was the illusion itself.

In late August of nineteen forty-four, the Ghost Army put on its first full performance.

American forces were attacking the port city of Brest, on the western tip of France. The Germans there were well dug in, and the Americans did not have as many troops nearby as they would have liked.

So the Ghost Army was asked to make them look bigger.

For about a week, the unit used everything it had. Rubber tanks appeared in the fields. Sound trucks played the noise of armor moving at night. Radio operators filled the air with the chatter of units that were not really there.

For the men, it was a test of whether all those months of training actually worked. Could a few hundred artists really make an enemy see an army that did not exist?

The answer, it turned out, was yes. And the Army began to call on them again… and again.

One of the most dangerous moments came in September of nineteen forty-four, in the small country of Luxembourg.

General Patton's Third Army was pushing east. But as the troops moved, a gap opened in the American line, a stretch of almost seventy miles that was barely defended.

If the Germans had discovered that gap, they could have poured through it and struck the American forces from behind.

There was no time to bring in a real division to fill it. So the Ghost Army was sent instead, disguised as the Sixth Armored Division.

For about a week, near a town called Bettembourg, a few hundred men pretended to be thousands. Inflatable tanks were set up in the woods. Radio operators played the part of a busy armored headquarters. And for four nights in a row, the sound trucks rolled out and filled the darkness with the noise of tanks on the move.

It was, in every sense, a bluff. If the Germans had attacked, there was almost nothing behind the curtain.

But they did not attack. The illusion held, until real troops could arrive and close the gap.

Even in the middle of all this, the men of the Ghost Army never stopped being artists.

When they were not inflating tanks or driving sound trucks, many of them were drawing. They sketched each other sleeping in barns. They painted the ruined churches of Normandy, the streets of Paris, the farm women of Luxembourg, the long gray roads of the winter of nineteen forty-four.

Hundreds of these sketches and watercolors survived the war. Today, they are some of the most personal records we have of what it felt like to be a young American soldier in Europe: not a heroic poster, but a tired boy with a pencil, trying to remember a place he would never see again.
