**A unidade**
- 23rd Headquarters Special Troops, a "Ghost Army". Ativada em **20/01/1944** em Camp Forrest, Tennessee. Cerca de **1.100 homens**.
- Unidades:
  - **603rd Engineer Camouflage Battalion**, ~379 homens (1 fonte para o número): engano visual com tanques, caminhões, artilharia e jipes infláveis;
  - **Signal Company Special**, ~296 homens (1 fonte para o número): "spoof radio", rádio falso;
  - **3132nd Signal Service Company**, ~145 homens (1 fonte para o número): engano sonoro.
    - Treinou em Pine Camp, Nova York.
    - Gravações feitas com engenheiros da Bell Labs, tocadas em alto-falantes de 500 W em meias-lagartas, audíveis a cerca de **15 milhas** (24 km).
- Artistas na unidade: **Bill Blass** (moda), **Ellsworth Kelly** (pintor), **Arthur Singer** (artista de vida selvagem), Art Kane, Victor Dowd, Jack Masey.
- Chegou à Europa por volta de maio/junho de 1944. Fez **mais de 20 operações**, da Normandia ao Reno.

**As operações**
- **Brest**, 20–27/08/1944: a primeira grande operação, com todos os tipos de engano.
- **Bettembourg**, Luxemburgo, 14–22/09/1944: a unidade fingiu ser a 6ª Divisão Blindada para cobrir uma brecha de quase **70 milhas** no front. O som de tanques tocou por quatro noites.
- **Viersen**, março de 1945: a maior operação. Os alemães acreditaram que o 9º Exército cruzaria o Reno em outro ponto e outra data.
  - Fingiram duas divisões, a 30ª e a 79ª (1 fonte: Wikipedia).

**Histórias e depois da guerra**
- Ciclistas franceses viram **quatro soldados levantando um tanque de "40 toneladas"**. Em várias fontes, um deles teria dito: "Os americanos são muito fortes!".
- A missão ficou **secreta até 1996**.
- A **Medalha de Ouro do Congresso** virou lei em 2022 e foi entregue em **21/03/2024**.
- Estimativa: **15 mil a 30 mil vidas salvas**. É uma estimativa histórica: na fala, dizer "historians estimate".

## Narration script (English; eleven_multilingual_v2 does not read tags, so the pauses are written as "…" and paragraph breaks)

### 1. Four men and a forty-ton tank
In the autumn of nineteen forty-four, two Frenchmen were riding their bicycles along a quiet country road, not far from the front lines.

Under a line of trees, they noticed something the war had made very familiar: American trucks, covered with netting… and behind them, a row of Sherman tanks.

An American sentry stepped out to wave them along. But the two men were not looking at him anymore. They were staring past his shoulder.

Because there, in the field, four young soldiers had walked up to one of those tanks… bent down… and lifted it off the ground.

A tank like that weighs more than thirty tons. And yet the four men carried it, turned it, and set it down again, as easily as you might move a kitchen table.

One of the Frenchmen turned to his friend and said, in complete seriousness: "These Americans… they are very strong."

The sentry did not correct him. He could not. Because what those two men had just seen was one of the best-kept secrets of the Second World War.

The tank was made of rubber. It was full of air. And the soldiers who carried it belonged to a unit that, officially, did not exist.

Today, we call them the Ghost Army. And for more than fifty years, almost nobody knew what they did.

### 2. An army of artists
To understand the Ghost Army, we have to go back to January of nineteen forty-four, to a training camp in Tennessee called Camp Forrest.

That winter, the United States Army quietly created a new unit with a long, forgettable name: the Twenty-Third Headquarters Special Troops.

It had about eleven hundred men. And it had one job, unlike any other job in the Army. It was not supposed to fight the enemy. It was supposed to fool him.

The idea was simple, and a little bit crazy. If you could make the Germans believe that a large American force was in one place… then the real army could move somewhere else, where it was not expected.

Wars are won and lost on information. Where is the enemy? How strong is he? Where will he strike next? The Ghost Army's mission was to make sure the Germans got all three answers wrong.

But to do that, the Army needed a very special kind of soldier.

So recruiters went looking in places the military does not usually visit: art schools in New York and Philadelphia, advertising agencies, design studios, theater companies.

They found painters and sculptors. Architects and set designers. Young men who knew how to make something look real… when it wasn't.

Among them was a young man from Indiana who loved fashion. His name was Bill Blass, and one day he would become one of the most famous designers in America.

There was a quiet young man named Ellsworth Kelly, who would later become one of the great painters of the twentieth century.

There was Arthur Singer, who would spend his life painting birds so precisely that his work filled field guides for generations.

And there were hundreds of others: men who would go on to become photographers, illustrators, art teachers and engineers.

Many of them had never imagined themselves in uniform. Now they were being trained to deceive the most powerful army in Europe… using paint, rubber, sound and imagination.

### 3. Rubber, sound and radio
The Ghost Army fought with three kinds of weapons. None of them could fire a single bullet.

The first weapon was what the enemy could see.

The largest part of the unit was the Six Hundred and Third Engineer Camouflage Battalion. These were the artists. Their tools were inflatable decoys: rubber tanks, trucks, jeeps and artillery guns, each one folded into a bag and blown up with a compressor in a matter of minutes.

From the air, through a pair of binoculars, or in a reconnaissance photograph, a field full of these decoys looked exactly like an armored division getting ready for battle.

But the artists knew that an army is more than its vehicles. Real tanks leave tracks in the mud. Real soldiers leave trash, laundry, and campfire smoke. So the men of the Ghost Army made those, too. They used vehicles, and even bulldozers, to draw tank tracks across the fields. They placed decoys with care, as if a real crew had parked them in a hurry.

The second weapon was what the enemy could hear.

A separate company, the Thirty-One Thirty-Second Signal Service Company, had trained in secret in upstate New York. Working with engineers from Bell Labs, they had recorded the sounds of real American units: tanks rolling, trucks shifting gears, men building bridges, officers shouting orders.

They mixed those sounds into programs, like a radio play, and stored them on wire recorders, the most advanced recording technology of the day.

At night, they drove half-tracks close to the front, carrying enormous five-hundred-watt speakers. And then they pressed play.

On a quiet night, those sounds could carry for miles, some say as far as fifteen miles. To a German soldier listening in the dark, it sounded like an entire armored column was moving into position.

The third weapon was what the enemy could intercept.

The Signal Company Special handled the radio. Every army listens to its enemy's radio traffic. The Ghost Army used that against the Germans. Its operators sent fake messages, imitating the real units they were pretending to be.

They even learned to copy the personal rhythm of other operators… the way each man tapped out Morse code, almost like handwriting. So that a German listener, hearing a familiar "hand" on the air, would never suspect a thing.

And there was a fourth weapon, maybe the most human of all: theater.

Members of the Ghost Army sewed on the shoulder patches of other divisions. They painted fake unit markings on their trucks. Some of them went into nearby towns and sat in cafés, talking a little too loudly about where "their division" was headed next, knowing that spies might be listening.

A few officers even played the part of generals, riding through villages with the right flags on their cars.

Sight. Sound. Radio. And a bit of acting. Put together, it was the first traveling, multimedia deception unit in the history of the United States Army.

### 4. Landing in France
In the spring of nineteen forty-four, the Ghost Army crossed the Atlantic. Some of its men landed in France in the weeks after D-Day, while the fighting in Normandy was still raging.

Their first missions were small, and they learned quickly. A decoy placed in the wrong spot could give the whole game away. A rubber tank left in the sun could slowly sag. A careless remark in a village could undo a week of work.

Every operation became a production, with a script, a set, a cast and a schedule. The men joked that they were a traveling road show. In a way, they were.

And their stages were never far from real danger. To be convincing, the Ghost Army often had to set up close to the front line, sometimes closer than the units they were imitating. They had almost no real weapons to defend themselves. Their protection was the illusion itself.

### 5. Brest: the first big show
In late August of nineteen forty-four, the Ghost Army put on its first full performance.

American forces were attacking the port city of Brest, on the western tip of France. The Germans there were well dug in, and the Americans did not have as many troops nearby as they would have liked.

So the Ghost Army was asked to make them look bigger.

For about a week, the unit used everything it had. Rubber tanks appeared in the fields. Sound trucks played the noise of armor moving at night. Radio operators filled the air with the chatter of units that were not really there.

For the men, it was a test of whether all those months of training actually worked. Could a few hundred artists really make an enemy see an army that did not exist?

The answer, it turned out, was yes. And the Army began to call on them again… and again.

### 6. Seventy miles of nothing
One of the most dangerous moments came in September of nineteen forty-four, in the small country of Luxembourg.

General Patton's Third Army was pushing east. But as the troops moved, a gap opened in the American line, a stretch of almost seventy miles that was barely defended.

If the Germans had discovered that gap, they could have poured through it and struck the American forces from behind.

There was no time to bring in a real division to fill it. So the Ghost Army was sent instead, disguised as the Sixth Armored Division.

For about a week, near a town called Bettembourg, a few hundred men pretended to be thousands. Inflatable tanks were set up in the woods. Radio operators played the part of a busy armored headquarters. And for four nights in a row, the sound trucks rolled out and filled the darkness with the noise of tanks on the move.

It was, in every sense, a bluff. If the Germans had attacked, there was almost nothing behind the curtain.

But they did not attack. The illusion held, until real troops could arrive and close the gap.

### 7. Sketchbooks in a war zone
Even in the middle of all this, the men of the Ghost Army never stopped being artists.

When they were not inflating tanks or driving sound trucks, many of them were drawing. They sketched each other sleeping in barns. They painted the ruined churches of Normandy, the streets of Paris, the farm women of Luxembourg, the long gray roads of the winter of nineteen forty-four.

Hundreds of these sketches and watercolors survived the war. Today, they are some of the most personal records we have of what it felt like to be a young American soldier in Europe: not a heroic poster, but a tired boy with a pencil, trying to remember a place he would never see again.

### 8. Viersen: the biggest bluff
By March of nineteen forty-five, the war in Europe was nearing its end. One great obstacle remained between the Allies and the heart of Germany: the Rhine River.

Crossing a river that wide, under fire, is one of the most dangerous things an army can attempt. If the Germans knew exactly where the crossing would happen, they could concentrate everything they had in that one place.

So the Ghost Army was given its largest and final mission, known as Operation Viersen.

Its task was to convince the Germans that the American Ninth Army would cross the Rhine at one place and time… when in fact it would cross somewhere else.

For this performance, the Ghost Army pulled out every trick it had learned. Hundreds of inflatable decoys. Fake headquarters. Radio traffic for whole divisions that were really somewhere else. Sound programs of bridge-building equipment and heavy vehicles at night. And this time, they had extra help from other units to make the show even bigger.

When the real crossing began, the resistance was lighter than many had feared. The Ninth Army crossed the Rhine, and the road into Germany was open.

Two months later, the war in Europe was over.

### 9. Fifty years of silence
When the men of the Ghost Army came home, they did something that might be the hardest part of their whole story: they said nothing.

Their work was classified. They were told not to talk about it, and they did not. Not to their friends. Often not even to their own wives and children.

They went back to their lives. They became painters, designers, architects, teachers, photographers. Some became famous. Most did not. And for decades, if anyone asked what they did in the war, many of them simply said they had been "in the engineers."

It was not until nineteen ninety-six that the Ghost Army's story was officially declassified. By then, many of its men had already passed away, their families never knowing the full truth.

In the years that followed, the families and researchers who helped bring the story to light began a long effort to have the unit honored.

In twenty twenty-two, a law was signed to award the Ghost Army the Congressional Gold Medal, the highest civilian honor Congress can give. And in March of twenty twenty-four, at a ceremony in Washington, the medal was finally presented. Only a handful of the veterans were still alive to see it.

### 10. What the ghosts left behind
How many lives did the Ghost Army save? We will never know exactly. Historians estimate the number somewhere between fifteen thousand and thirty thousand. Fifteen to thirty thousand young men who came home, because the enemy was looking the wrong way at the right moment.

And maybe that is the most remarkable thing about this story. The Ghost Army did not win its battles with weapons. It won them with imagination, with the talents of people who, in any other time, would have spent their lives painting, designing, and making beautiful things.

So the next time you see an old watercolor of a French village, or a dress by Bill Blass, or a painting by Ellsworth Kelly in a museum… remember the rubber tanks in the fields of France.

And remember those two Frenchmen on their bicycles, watching four young Americans lift a tank into the air.

They were right, in a way. Those Americans were very strong. Just not in the way anyone imagined.
